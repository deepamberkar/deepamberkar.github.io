Give every user a dashboard of their own

Self-service usage is the cheapest cost control there is.

Most conversations about AI spend are conversations about administrators looking at users. The most effective control I've found works the other way round: give every person a page of their own, and let them watch themselves.

What's on it

  • Spend this month against their own budget, in money.
  • A month-end forecast at the current pace, so nobody is surprised on the last day.
  • What caching saved them, or cost them, because sometimes it costs and the page should say so.
  • Where the money went, by model and by type of work.
  • Their sessions, so they can see which of their tools spends the most.
  • The models they may use, with prices next to them.
  • What was refused, and why.
  • Any earlier month this year, not just the current one.

Why it works

People change behaviour when they can see it. A forecast is a friendly conversation with yourself on the twentieth; a refusal is an unfriendly one with an administrator on the thirtieth. A person who can see that one long session cost more than the rest of the week combined needs no policy document to adjust.

It also removes a whole class of support requests. "Why is my usage so high?" and "which model should I be using?" are answered by a page, not by a person.

The honest details

The unglamorous states matter most. A person who hasn't used anything yet this month should see a clear, friendly empty page with their own name on it, not a blank and not an identifier. Someone with no cap should be told their spend is measured, not limited. A figure that isn't final should say how fresh it is. Usage that couldn't be priced should be flagged, never quietly counted as free.

Designing for the three-second look

Most people will look at this page for a few seconds, once or twice a month. So the first thing they see answers the first question, am I on track?, and everything else is there for the person who wants to dig. A page built for the five percent who love dashboards fails the ninety-five who just want to know whether they're fine.

What it deliberately isn't

It isn't a league table, and it isn't a surveillance tool. People see their own page; only administrators can open someone else's. The aim is self-awareness, not shaming, and a page that felt like being watched would be switched off by the first person who noticed.

Same page, different viewer

An administrator can open any person's page and see exactly what that person sees. That sounds minor and it's the opposite: "why is mine so high?" gets answered by two people looking at the same screen, with the same numbers, instead of two people comparing screenshots.

One set of numbers

The personal page isn't a separate product with its own arithmetic. It's another view of the same records that feed the executive reports, the budgets and the health view. If a person's page and a manager's report ever disagreed, the product would have failed at its one job. That's the clearest argument I know for treating the gateway and the portal as a single product.

The best cost control is a person who can see their own spend.
