When the gateway says no, say why, and offer a way forward

Refusals, and the "I need more" button.

Every gateway says no sometimes. Over budget. Model not allowed. Not yet onboarded. The question is what the person sees next. A bare error teaches them nothing, produces a support ticket, and makes the product the villain.

Say why

Each refusal carries a reason in plain words and, where there is one, what to do next. The reasons are recorded too, so an administrator can see a pattern: a model people keep asking for that isn't allowed, a group who were never onboarded, a limit that is simply too low. A refusal is information the product should keep, not just something it did.

Not all refusals are the same

A good refusal tells you which situation you're in. Limit reached: you can ask for more. Not allowed: you can ask for access. Not set up yet: tell your administrator. Service trouble: it isn't your fault, and you shouldn't have to wonder. Four causes, four next steps, and a person who can tell them apart stops raising tickets that say only "it doesn't work".

The "I need more" button

Close to their limit, a person can ask for more with one click. The administrator sees who asked and how much, and decides: approve the amount asked, approve a different amount, or deny. An administrator can also grant relief directly, without waiting for a request, when they already know the answer.

What relief is, and isn't

Relief raises that month's allowance. It never erases what was spent, so the spend figure stays honest, and it applies to that month only: next month the normal budget returns. That keeps two promises at once. The person gets unblocked, and the books still show exactly what happened.

For the person deciding

An administrator reviewing the queue needs context, not just a name: how much was asked, how much has been spent, how the person usually behaves. A decision made in ten seconds with the right facts beats one made in ten minutes with a spreadsheet, and it's the difference between relief being a service and relief being a chore.

Everything leaves a trail

Who asked, when, how much, who decided and what they decided are all kept, and administrator actions go into an audit log. Only senior roles can approve or deny; others can see the queue but not change it.

There's a view of the relief process itself, too: how many people are asking, what share are approved, how much has been granted, how that's trending month on month, and who is blocked right now. That last view is more useful than it sounds. If grants rise every month, the base budgets are wrong, and now you can see it.

Why on-demand matters

The alternatives are both bad. Set limits high enough that nobody ever hits them and you have no control. Set them where you want them and refuse people, and they stop using the tool, or find a way round it. On-demand relief is control that bends instead of breaking: firm by default, flexible by decision, and transparent either way.

For anyone moving Claude access to their own cloud, this is often the first thing the business asks for after "can we see who's using it": what happens when someone hits the wall at a bad moment?

A limit people can't ask to change will eventually be worked around.
