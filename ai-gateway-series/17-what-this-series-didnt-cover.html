What this series didn't cover

One scope, drawn on purpose.

Sixteen posts about one slice of a platform can leave the wrong impression: that this is everything an AI platform needs. It isn't. Before you go, here is what I left out, and why the line sits where it does.

The line I drew

This series is about one layer: the governance layer between the applications people use and the model backends behind them. It decides who may ask, counts what was spent, applies limits, and explains afterwards what happened. Every post was an argument about that layer, not about a whole platform. That scope was deliberate: narrow enough to describe honestly, wide enough to matter.

The five pillars, honestly

The Azure Well-Architected Framework looks at a workload through five pillars: reliability, security, cost optimization, operational excellence and performance efficiency. Holding the series against them is a useful and slightly humbling exercise.

  • Cost optimization got real attention: budgets, honest metering, and what a gateway costs to run.
  • Operational excellence got some: health across the whole path, and proving a change works.
  • Security appeared mainly through identity. Network design, data handling, content controls and threat modelling deserve their own series.
  • Reliability, availability and scalability got the offline-mode post and little more. Zones, regions, failover and recovery are a large subject I haven't done justice to.
  • Performance efficiency, meaning latency, throughput and capacity under load, was barely mentioned.

I'd rather say so than let a polished series imply a complete architecture. If you build one, the last three need as much design time as the parts I wrote about.

What gateways are being asked to carry next

The ground is moving. Agents don't only call models; they call tools, and sometimes each other. Three things keep coming up:

  • Tool servers, reached through the Model Context Protocol (MCP), which let a model act on other systems.
  • Agent-to-agent communication (A2A), where one agent hands work to another.
  • Shared repositories of skills and reusable instructions that agents load on demand.

Each is a new kind of consumer or provider on the path, and each raises the questions this series kept asking: who is calling, what may they do, what did it cost, what went wrong. Whether one gateway should govern them, or something alongside it should, is an open question without a settled answer yet. I'm watching it, not claiming it.

Why I'm comfortable with a narrow scope

A governance layer that tries to be everything stops being easy to reason about. The version I'd defend is the narrow one: a clear door, one set of rules, honest numbers and a control room that can explain itself. Anything on that list can be built around it, or added to it, without changing what it is for.

If you're planning one

Treat this series as one input to a review, not a checklist that finishes the job. Put the five pillars on a page, write next to each what your design already answers and what it leaves open, and be as candid about the blanks as about the strengths. The blanks are where the next conversation should start.

Govern the path between applications and models, and say plainly what you left out.
