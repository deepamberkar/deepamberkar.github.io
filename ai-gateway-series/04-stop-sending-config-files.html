Stop sending people a config file

Letting clients discover what they're allowed to use.

The old way to onboard a new user was a document. Here's the endpoint. Here are the model names. Here's how to set up your client. Three weeks later the model list changes and every document in every inbox is wrong.

I wanted clients to ask. "What can I use?" And the product answers: here are the models you can use, here's how to use them, and nothing else.

Why it matters

  • Setup becomes "point at the gateway and sign in." That's the whole onboarding.
  • The catalogue has one owner. Change it once and every client sees the change.
  • People don't see what they can't use. That removes a whole class of "why does this fail?" tickets.

What it replaces

Before discovery, adding a model meant a message to everyone, an updated settings file, a reminder a week later, and a steady trickle of people on last month's list wondering why the new model "doesn't exist". Multiply by every tool a person uses, and "configuration" becomes a part-time job for whoever is unlucky enough to own it.

With discovery, a client such as Claude Desktop simply asks the product what's available to the person using it, and shows that. Nobody edits a file. Nobody re-sends an email. The model list is a living thing, not a document.

One catalogue, owned in one place

The catalogue is managed in the portal and answered by the gateway, which is the one-product idea in miniature. The portal owns what exists and who may see what. The gateway owns the moment of asking. If the two halves disagreed, a person would see a model they couldn't call, or couldn't see one they should have. Because it's one product, they can't drift apart.

A security note hiding in a convenience feature

A list of models you aren't allowed to use is information too. Discovery isn't just a convenience; it's another surface. Filter before you answer, never after. And treat a discovery request like any other request: it has an identity, it is checked, and it is recorded.

Discovery also has to stay out of the way of the numbers. A client asking "what can I use?" on every start-up shouldn't count as usage, shouldn't skew a health verdict, and shouldn't cost anyone a cent. It's the kind of detail that's invisible when right and embarrassing when wrong.

What I'd tell you to check

If you build discovery, test it with two real people who should see different things, and confirm they do. Test a person who should see nothing, and confirm they get a clean, polite empty answer rather than an error. And test what happens when the catalogue changes while a client is open. Those three cases find most of the problems.

It's a small feature with a large effect on how the whole product feels. Onboarding that takes a minute changes how people talk about a tool, and how many of them bother to try it.

Who it works for

Claude Desktop, and any other consumer that knows how to ask. Nobody has to hand-configure a model list again, and nobody's list drifts out of date silently. For an administrator it's the difference between maintaining a catalogue and maintaining a distribution list of everyone who has a copy of it.

The best onboarding document is no document.
