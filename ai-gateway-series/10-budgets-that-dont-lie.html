Budgets that don't lie

Soft limits, hard limits, and why money beats tokens.

Tokens are the unit the models count in. They're the wrong unit for the people paying. A token costs different amounts on different models, going in versus coming out, fresh versus cached. A limit expressed in tokens is a limit nobody can explain to finance.

So the first decision: budget in money. A person understands "you have this much to spend this month" without a glossary.

Two halves of one budget

A budget has two jobs, and they live in different halves of the product. The portal is where a budget is set, explained and watched: who has what, how much is left, who is close. The gateway is where it is applied, on the very next request, without anyone having to be awake. If either half is missing you have either a rule nobody can see, or a report nobody can act on.

Two kinds of limit

A soft limit tells you. A hard limit stops you. Most systems ship only the hard one, which means the first time a person hears about their budget is the moment their work stops. A good product talks before it enforces.

Be honest about what you know

Any budget figure is slightly out of date by the time you read it. Say how old it is. And decide, in advance and out loud, what the system does when it can't check: let people through, or stop them? Neither is wrong. An undecided answer is.

The same honesty applies to the number itself. A budget is only as good as the records behind it, which is why the money in this product comes from one place, written once, and read by everything else. A budget bar that disagrees with a cost report is worse than no budget bar at all.

Different people, different limits

People rarely share one allowance. A researcher, a contractor and an application don't need the same ceiling, and pretending otherwise just moves the argument to a support ticket. The trick is making the differences explainable in a sentence, because the day someone asks "why is mine lower?", you want a sentence, not an archaeology project.

The most specific rule wins: a personal allowance first, then the one for a person's tier, then the organisation's default. Real life also produces exceptions, such as a deadline, a one-off experiment or a demo. How a person asks for more matters as much as the limit itself, so that has its own post.

Where it goes wrong

The commonest failure isn't a bad limit; it's a surprising one. A person hits a wall they didn't know existed, on the day they needed the tool most, and the support request that follows is really a complaint about trust. Almost every design choice here exists to prevent that moment: budgets in money, a heads-up zone, an honest "as of" time, and a clear message that says what happened and who to ask.

A limit that is fair but invisible will feel unfair. A limit that is visible but arbitrary will be argued with. You need both properties at once.

What good feels like

The best budget system is one most people never think about. They get a quiet nudge as they approach a limit, a clear message if they reach it, and a person to ask if they need more. Nobody is surprised, and nobody has to read a policy document to understand why.

A budget the user can't see coming is a punishment, not a control.
